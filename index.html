Chem 297 · Environmental Chemistry

Superfund Site Profile: NC State University Lot 86, Farm Unit #1

The scenario

A real 1.5-acre Superfund site on the NC State campus in Raleigh, where science lab and agricultural research waste was buried in trenches between 1969 and 1980. The EPA added it to the National Priorities List in 1986.

What I did

Used EPA site profiles and Five-Year Review records to trace the site from start to finish: what was dumped (solvents, pesticides, heavy metals, acids, PCBs, PAHs, and low-level radioactive material), which media were contaminated (soil and groundwater), and how the cleanup worked. The EPA's 1996 plan treated and capped the soil, which was finished in 1999, and extracted and treated the groundwater, with that system completed in 2006. Land use and groundwater restrictions and a fence remain in place.

What I took from it

The chemistry explains the site's behavior. Volatile organic compounds like TCE do not bind well to soil, so they travel far in groundwater, while PCBs and PAHs break down slowly and can linger for decades. I also looked at what could have happened without a cleanup, including TCE exposure through drinking water or vapor in buildings, with more than 200,000 people living within five miles. The 2023 review found the site now meets all four Superfund performance measures.

ENHS 340 · Environmental Technology and Control

Sampling and Analysis Plan for Hexavalent Chromium

The scenario

A hypothetical chrome plating facility needed a field plan for finding hexavalent chromium (Cr (VI)) in groundwater, workplace air, and surface soil.

What I did

Wrote a step-by-step sampling procedure for each medium: low-flow groundwater sampling with field filtering and a pH 9 to 9.5 buffer (EPA Method 218.6), breathing-zone air sampling on a 37 mm filter (NIOSH Method 7600), and surface soil sampling with alkaline digestion at the lab (EPA SW-846 Method 3060A). I also set up sample ID formats and chain-of-custody tables so field staff have what they need to hand samples to an accredited lab.

What I took from it

Cr (VI) is chemically unstable, so timing and preservation matter. Regulation is also uneven across media. OSHA sets a workplace air limit of 5 µg/m³ as an 8-hour average, but I found no enforceable federal or North Carolina soil standard, and the lab service list I used had no Cr (VI) air analysis, so I built that part from NIOSH and OSHA sources.

ENHS 340 · Environmental Technology and Control

Chromium Release Report and Cleanup Field Notes

The scenario

In a follow-up scenario at the same hypothetical plating facility, a forklift punctured drums of chromic acid, and about 6,000 pounds of Cr (VI) reached the soil over roughly 24 hours before anyone noticed.

What I did

Wrote the release report: the quantity was above the 5,000 pound CERCLA reportable quantity, so it called for National Response Center, LEPC, and state notification. I also assigned the RCRA waste code D007 through TCLP testing, listed first responder precautions from the Emergency Response Guidebook, and laid out field and lab detection methods. Then I wrote cleanup field notes for a technician: dig out the worst surface soil, treat it with ferrous sulfate to turn Cr (VI) into the less toxic Cr (III), and treat deeper soil in place with in situ chemical reduction.

What I took from it

A cleanup plan only works if the people around it understand it. I added plain-language talking points and sample questions from an owner and a neighbor, including why hauling everything away would only move the chromium somewhere else. Tying the treatment choices to the waste management hierarchy kept the reasoning clear.

ENHS 320 · Incident Response and Investigation

Propane Incident Investigations

The scenario

Two real incidents, both involving propane: a fatal flash fire in a Nebraska hog farrowing shed (Nebraska FACE Investigation 02NE004) and the 2007 Little General Store explosion in Ghent, West Virginia (CSB Report 2007-04-I-WV).

What I did

For the farm fire, I completed an incident investigation form covering the sequence of events and sorted the causes into superficial and underlying. A cracked shut-off valve was the surface cause. Taking the cracked valve off and reconnecting the pipe with no shut-off, and running a ventilation fan on low, were the deeper ones. For the Ghent explosion, I ran five analyses on the same incident: a timeline, a root cause analysis, a what-if analysis, Five Whys, and a cause-and-effect tree. Then I ranked my recommendations.

What I took from it

The Five Whys and the root cause analysis kept landing on the same roots, mostly gaps in training and supervision, which showed me how the methods back each other up. My top recommendation was formal certification and enforced supervision for propane technicians, followed by tighter tank installation audits and annual propane-specific training for responders. The what-if analysis was the most useful to me, since it asks which safeguards should have caught the problem earlier.

ENHS 320 · Incident Response and Investigation

Emergency Action and Business Continuity Plan

The scenario

Using lessons from the Ghent explosion, I built a plan framework for a fictional small-town convenience store with a propane tank and cylinder exchange.

What I did

Chose an Emergency Action Plan under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.38, since store employees are not trained to stop a propane leak or fight a propane fire. The core rule is evacuate first: employees report, call 911, get everyone out, and account for people. The plan scales through three response levels, from a minor outside leak to a fire or explosion, and ties each part to the OSHA, EPA, and NFPA requirements that apply. I also added business continuity steps, since a small-town store may be the only nearby source of fuel, groceries, and propane.

What I took from it

The Ghent investigation turned into planning rules. Vapor built up for about 28 minutes with nobody evacuating, so the plan treats any propane release as an evacuation. Writing it showed me how an investigation's findings become procedures people can actually follow.

WRTG 393 · Advanced Technical Writing

Cybersecurity Risk Report for a Small Hotel

The scenario

A fictional small hotel with an open WiFi network, no firewall or antivirus, secondhand 2013 security cameras still on their factory-default logins, and no technology policy or staff training.

What I did

Wrote a report to the hotel owner from the point of view of a front desk employee. It opens with an executive summary, explains three problems, and pairs each one with a low-cost fix: password-protect the WiFi, split guest and business networks, and add a firewall, antivirus, and automatic updates. Change the camera logins right away and plan to replace the cameras. Create a one-page technology policy and give a short annual training. I used tables and a figure to compare what the hotel does now with what it should do.

What I took from it

The reader was a business owner, not an IT person, so every risk got an everyday comparison and every problem got a fix that is cheap and quick. Several of the steps can be done in a single afternoon. It showed me how to turn a technical risk into a decision someone can act on.
